I built Arbiter on my own in five days for the Open Agent Hackathon 2026, sponsored by NVIDIA and
SpaceX, in the Reasoning Architecture track. I competed solo against thousands of participants, most
of them in teams, and Arbiter placed 28th in the track.

It is for the third-party risk analyst who decides whether a new SaaS vendor may touch customer
data. The vendor answers a security questionnaire; Arbiter checks each answer against what the
company's own systems say, from contracts and incidents to watchlists and CISA's list of
known-exploited vulnerabilities, and does the first pass of the triage.

Five agents do the reasoning on NVIDIA Nemotron, through Meterless Nightshift. A Planner chooses
which records to read, an Evidence agent extracts each claim with a verbatim quote, an Evaluator
checks the claims against the records and sends the plan back when evidence is missing, a Challenger
attacks the candidate decision, and a Reporter writes the cited brief. The decision itself is made
by a versioned policy engine, not a model: one of five outcomes, from approve to decline. When
evidence is missing, Arbiter drafts the request back to the vendor.

All five company systems sit behind one Zetaris semantic layer, so the agents query one governed
model instead of five schemas. Meterless H-MEM recalls precedents a TPRM lead has approved, and
Meterless Scout screens vendor text before any model sees it. Every step is written to a hash-chained
audit log, every action needs a signed execution contract, and any decision can be replayed from the
log.

## What it does

A third-party risk analyst at a 500 to 5,000-person company decides whether a new SaaS vendor may
touch customer data, and a TPRM lead signs off the exceptions. The evidence sits in five systems and
a questionnaire, and the queue does not wait:

- Third parties are involved in 48% of breaches
  ([Verizon 2026 DBIR](https://www.verizon.com/business/resources/Te26/reports/2026-dbir-data-breach-investigations-report.pdf)).
- Organisations average 2,643 third parties but assess only 36% of them, and 40% have an assessment
  backlog
  ([ProcessUnity / Ponemon-Sullivan 2026, n = 1,465](https://ponemonsullivanreport.com/2026/03/state-of-third-party-risk-assessments/)).
- Companies spend 37.4 hours a week assessing vendors
  ([Whistic 2025 TPRM Impact Report, n = 525](https://www.whistic.com/resources/blog/2025-impact-report-takeaways)).

Arbiter turns one onboarding into a cited, replayable decision with one of five outcomes:

- Approve
- Approve with conditions
- Request information
- Escalate
- Decline

## How it reasons

The orchestrator is an explicit state machine, and each agent has a narrow job:

| Agent             | Can                                                                             | Cannot                                    |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| `PlannerAgent`    | Choose registry queries from the USL catalogue; revise the plan                 | Write SQL, or skip a required control     |
| `EvidenceAgent`   | Run registry queries, run ScoutGuard, extract claims with verbatim quotes       | Mark a claim verified                     |
| `EvaluatorAgent`  | Ground quotes, cross-check claims against systems of record, send the plan back | Decide an outcome                         |
| `PolicyEngine`    | Tier the vendor; produce the outcome and what evidence would change it          | Call a model                              |
| `MemoryKeeper`    | Retrieve lead-approved precedents; record feedback                              | Change the policy                         |
| `ChallengerAgent` | Attack the candidate decision; request one re-plan; escalate                    | Approve or upgrade an outcome             |
| `ReporterAgent`   | Write the cited brief and draft the next action                                 | Cite anything outside the decision record |
| `ScoutGuard`      | Block, warn or pass untrusted vendor text; redact PII                           | Decide                                    |
| `ActionExecutor`  | Issue and verify signed contracts; run the outbox once approvals exist          | Act without a valid contract              |

## Credits

Arbiter runs on its sponsors' technology:

- NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 Super VL 120B and Nemotron 3.5 Lightning 30B, through Meterless Nightshift;
- Meterless H-MEM and Scout;
- the Zetaris Unified Semantic Layer;
- the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue, as a committed snapshot.

One deviation, stated plainly, as Arbiter's README states it. The deep lane was designed for
Nemotron 3 Super, which the account was not entitled to. A Nightshift routing rule coerced both lanes
to `auto`, so lane separation was not guaranteed, and Meterless acknowledged the routing bug on 24
October.

I built Arbiter with coding agents, Cursor's for the early passes and then Claude Code, and reviewed
every change, as the README's "AI and model usage" records.
